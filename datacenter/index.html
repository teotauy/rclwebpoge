In plain words

It is a warehouse full of computers

That is all it is. Every photo you have texted, every video you have watched and every bank balance you have checked sits on a computer somewhere. A data center is the building where thousands of those computers live together, so they can be kept cool, powered and connected.

Think of the library in town. Now picture one that never closes, has no readers inside, and is hundreds of times bigger. Only a few people work there. Mostly it is machines, running day and night.

    Why so many, so fast?

    Chatbots and other AI need far more computing than anything before them. The newest buildings are being made for that. A single large one can use as much electricity as tens of thousands of homes.

    That is why companies are racing to build, and why small towns are being asked to say yes.

    Why it gets hot

    Every computer is a small space heater. Put tens of thousands of them in one building and you have an enormous heater that must be cooled every second, or the machines burn out.

    Cooling is where most of the water and a good share of the electricity goes.

    What it brings

    Traffic and crashes

    The heavy traffic comes while the building is going up, not after it opens. For a year or two, hundreds or even thousands of workers drive in every day, along with dump trucks, concrete mixers and flatbeds.     What happened in rural Louisiana

    Around Meta’s giant data center site in Richland Parish, police records show 64 crashes from January to mid-September 2025, compared with 9 in all of 2024. State counts in front of Holly Ridge Elementary rose from 1,817 vehicles on a school day in 2022 to 5,224 in July 2025, and the school closed its playground over truck safety. About 5,000 workers were expected at the peak, for about 500 permanent jobs. Meta says it sets strict speed and safety rules and is putting more than $200 million into local infrastructure. Read the report.

    Similar complaints have been reported near sites in Iowa, Oklahoma, Michigan and Ohio. It is not the same everywhere: reporting from Plain Township, Ohio said crashes there had not gone up, though neighbors still complained of congestion. No one has yet done a careful study across many sites, so treat Louisiana as the worst well-documented case, not the average.

    What a good traffic plan includes

    • A written truck route that keeps heavy vehicles off school, village and neighborhood roads.
    • Police or flaggers at the busy intersections, paid for by the company. In Saline Township, Michigan, Oracle said it pays for state police patrols.
    • Staggered shift times, and parking away from the site with shuttles.
    • Turn lanes and signals built before the heavy work starts, not after the first crash.
    • A bond or agreement that the company repairs the roads it wears out.

    Good neighbor or bad neighbor?

    It depends almost entirely on the deal the town signs. Same building, very different results. Here is the worst case beside the good deal, topic by topic.

    What they do get right

    A data center sends no children to the school and puts few cars on the road once it is open, though building it is another story. It pays a very large tax bill, often more than the farm or factory it replaces, and that money can fix roads and fund schools. It is quiet to look at compared with most heavy industry. The trouble is rarely the building. It is usually the secrecy, the length of the tax break, and who ends up paying for the power.

    What else could we do?

    Could it be done differently?

    Say we all agree we want the good stuff: faster medical scans, better weather forecasts, a video call with the grandkids. The question becomes how to get it with the least trouble. There are real ideas, and none is a magic fix. Pick what matters most to you.

    What matters most?

    Far-out ideas

    Underwater. Microsoft sank a sealed data center off Scotland as a test, so the sea could cool it. It worked, but they did not build more.

    In orbit. Some companies have floated the idea of computers in space, running on sunshine. It is unproven, and shedding heat in a vacuum is hard.

    Treat these as science projects, not plans for your town.

    The honest part

    Efficiency gains often get spent on doing more, so total use can keep climbing even when each task gets cheaper. And a trick that works in Finland may not fit your county.

    The best deals mix several of these, and put them in writing. That is a good question for the town meeting: which of these has the company actually promised?
