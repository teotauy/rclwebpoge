    What the napkin chart skips

    1. Old records mean royalty points, not ownership.The 90s masters belong to the label (Virgin, now part of Universal). Band members earn a royalty rate on them once the label has earned back its costs.
    2. US AM/FM radio pays songwriters only.A spin on a US terrestrial station pays the label and the performers nothing. Satellite and internet radio do pay performers, through SoundExchange.
    3. James Iha has real writing credits.He co-wrote "Mayonaise", "Soma" and "I Am One", and wrote and sang "Blew Away" and "Take Me Down".
    4. Who played it isn't who gets paid.Corgan has said he tracked most of the guitar and bass on Siamese Dream. Royalties still follow the band's agreement, not the session notes.
    5. Hired players get paid on bad nights too.If a show loses money, the partners cover it. Push production costs up on the tour tab to see it.
    6. Merch carries a lot of tours.After the venue's cut and the cost of the shirts, the band keeps a bigger share of a T-shirt than of a ticket.

    An illustration for curiosity, built from public industry rules of thumb. It isn't the band's actual books, contracts or splits, and it isn't financial or legal advice. (Please don't be mad, Billy.)

    In October of 2026, I walked from my apartment to the Barclays Center in Brooklyn and saw the Smashing Pumpkins perform "Today." I cried.

    Somewhere in the middle I started thinking about how they were probably the perfect case study for how bands get paid. A former member (D'arcy). A former member becoming a member again (James). Touring musicians (Kiki).

    These numbers are kind of made up, based on best guesses. This is way more of a thought starter. I found it interesting, but I nerd out about a lot.
